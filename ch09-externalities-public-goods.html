When the market gets the quantity wrong

A competitive market is efficient only if every cost and benefit falls on the buyer or the seller. When some of it falls on bystanders, or when people can't be made to pay, the market produces the wrong amount.

Costs and benefits that land on someone else

chunk 1 of 4

Chapter 6 showed that a competitive market maximises surplus. That result has a condition attached: every cost and every benefit has to fall on the buyer or the seller. When some of it falls on bystanders, the market gets the quantity wrong.

Negative externalityPositive externality
What spills overa cost: pollution, noise, congestiona benefit: vaccination, education, a restored façade
Which curve is wrongsocial cost = private cost + external costsocial benefit = private benefit + external benefit
Market producestoo much: Qmarket > Qoptimaltoo little: Qmarket < Qoptimal
Classic fixa tax equal to the external costa subsidy equal to the external benefit
"Pollution should be zero"

The efficient amount of a negative externality is almost never zero. It is the amount at which the social cost of the last unit equals its benefit. Banning a good with an external cost throws away all the units that were worth making even after the damage is counted. The aim is the right quantity, not none at all.

Making people pay for what they cause

chunk 2 of 4

The fix is called internalising the externality: making the people who decide how much to buy or produce feel the whole cost or benefit. There are three main ways to do it.

  • Corrective (Pigouvian) tax or subsidy. Set it equal to the external cost or benefit per unit. The market then reaches the efficient quantity without anyone choosing that quantity directly. You can check this in the model above.
  • Regulation. Mandate a quantity, a technology or an emissions cap. This works when the harm is severe or easy to measure. It is usually more expensive than a tax, because it doesn't let the firms that can cut cheaply do most of the cutting.
  • Private bargaining (the Coase theorem). If property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the parties will negotiate to the efficient outcome on their own. That happens whichever side holds the right. The assignment of rights decides who pays whom. It doesn't decide the quantity.
When Coase fails

Bargaining breaks down when transaction costs are high. That happens when there are many parties (a city breathing the same air), when it's hard to identify who caused what, or when property rights are unclear. Two neighbours arguing over a barking dog can settle it privately. Millions of people affected by a power plant can't.

Over-correcting is also a mistake

A tax larger than the external cost pushes quantity below the optimum and creates a new deadweight loss. Set the externality to $15 and the tax to $30 in the model to see it. The right tax is the one that equals the externality, not the biggest one.

Four kinds of goods, and the two that markets struggle with

chunk 3 of 4

Two questions sort every good. Rival: does my using it leave less for you? Excludable: can someone who didn't pay be kept from using it?

ExcludableNon-excludable
RivalPrivate goods: a sandwich, a pair of jeansCommon resources: ocean fish, groundwater, a free public road at rush hour
Non-rivalClub goods: streaming services, cable TV, a toll road with no trafficPublic goods: national defence, fireworks displays, lighthouses
  • Public goods → the free-rider problem. Nobody can be excluded, so each person hopes someone else pays. The good isn't provided even when it is worth far more than it costs.
  • Common resources → the tragedy of the commons. Each user takes as much as they want, but each unit taken leaves less for everyone else. That cost is a negative externality on the other users, so the resource is overused until it runs out. The fixes are the ones you'd expect: quotas, user fees, or turning it into private property.
"Provided by the government" doesn't make something a public good

Public schools, the post office and public housing are provided by government, but they are rival and excludable. The classification is about the properties of the good, not about who supplies it. A road can even switch categories: it is a public good when it's empty and a common resource when it's jammed.

What the exam actually asks

chunk 4 of 4

Exam questions on this chapter follow a few fixed patterns. Each has a trap that graders watch for.

Asked asDo thisTrap
"Does the market produce too much or too little?"Negative → too much. Positive → too little. Say which curve the market ignores. Saying the market "can't find equilibrium". It finds one fine. It's just the wrong one.
"Find the efficient quantity"Add the externality to the right curve, then set social benefit = social cost. Adding an external cost to demand. External costs belong on the cost side.
"What tax would fix it?"A per-unit tax equal to the external cost per unit. Proposing a ban, or a tax equal to the total damage.
"Classify the good"Ask both questions: rival? excludable? Then name the box. Classifying by who provides it.
"Why won't the market provide it?"Non-excludable → free riders → too little private funding. Saying "because it's too expensive". Cost isn't the problem. Collecting payment is.
The idea that ties it together

Externalities, public goods and common resources are all the same failure: some cost or benefit doesn't reach the person making the decision. A free rider receives a positive externality from whoever pays. An overfisher imposes a negative externality on the other fishers. When you're stuck on a question, ask whose cost or benefit is being left out.
