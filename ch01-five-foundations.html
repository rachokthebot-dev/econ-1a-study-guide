Scarcity is the reason economics exists

chunk 1 of 6

One idea and two vocabulary splits that show up on every exam in the course.

Economics is the study of how people allocate scarce resources. That is the whole subject. The engine underneath it is scarcity.

The trap everyone falls into first

Scarcity does not mean "rare." Water is abundant and still scarce — you cannot have unlimited amounts at zero cost. A signed photo of a band nobody likes is rare and not scarce, because nobody wants it. Rare is about supply; scarce is about supply relative to wants.

So every society answers the same three questions: what to produce, how, and who gets it. The rest of the course is machinery for answering those.

Two splits you will be asked to make

SplitOne sideOther sideHow to tell
Scale Micro — individual decision-makers: a household, a firm, one market Macro — the economy as a whole: inflation, unemployment, growth Count the actors. One market or one chooser → micro.
Claim type Positive — testable, factual: "a $15 minimum wage reduces teen employment" Normative — value judgement: "a $15 minimum wage is fair" Could data settle it? Yes → positive. Words like should, fair, ought → normative.

Positive statements can be wrong and still be positive — "the earth is flat" is a positive statement. The test is whether evidence could decide it, not whether the claim is true.

Foundation 1 — Incentives

chunk 2 of 6

People respond to incentives, predictably, and often in ways the person designing the incentive did not plan. This foundation is where most exam questions about "unintended consequences" come from.

An incentive is anything that changes the costs or benefits of an action. Two classifications matter, and exam questions almost always turn on the second one.

  • Positive vs negative. Positive incentives make an action more attractive (a rebate, a bonus, a scholarship). Negative incentives make it less attractive (a fine, a tax, a late fee). Both work by changing cost–benefit.
  • Direct vs indirect. A direct incentive gives the result you were aiming at. An indirect incentive is the side effect nobody put in the press release. Indirect incentives are where the interesting economics lives.
Worked example — seat belts

Mandatory seat belts have a direct effect: a given crash is more survivable, so deaths per crash fall. They also have an indirect effect: feeling safer, some drivers drive a little faster and follow a little closer, so the number of crashes rises — including crashes involving pedestrians and cyclists, who got no seat belt. The net effect on total deaths is an empirical question, and that is exactly the point. Identifying the indirect incentive is the skill being tested; you are not expected to know the net sign from memory.

Foundation 2 — Trade-offs

chunk 3 of 6

Scarcity forces choice, and choice means giving something up. It gets its own foundation because students collapse it into opportunity cost, and the exam distinguishes them.

There is no such thing as a free lunch. Every choice costs you the alternatives — including the ones that feel free. A free concert costs you the three hours.

Trade-off vs opportunity cost — the distinction

A trade-off is the fact that choosing one thing means giving up others. It is plural and qualitative: "studying tonight means no gym, no party, no shift at work."

An opportunity cost is the single highest-valued alternative you gave up, and it carries a number. "Studying tonight cost me the $80 shift" — the one you'd actually have picked.

Trade-offs are the menu of things forgone. Opportunity cost is the best item on that menu. If a question says "list the trade-offs" and you name one thing, you lost points; if it says "what is the opportunity cost" and you name three, you also lost points.

Trade-offs are why the production possibilities curve in Chapter 3 slopes downward and why budget constraints in Chapter 4 are straight lines. When you meet those, you are meeting this foundation with axes attached.

Foundation 3 — Opportunity cost

chunk 4 of 6

The most tested idea in the chapter. Three rules cover every version of it you will see.

Opportunity cost is the value of the highest-valued alternative you gave up. Three rules follow, and each one is a graded exam trap.

Rule 1 — Count implicit costs, not just the money

Explicit costs are payments you actually make: tuition, rent on a shop, wages to employees. Implicit costs are the value of resources you already own and use up: the salary you did not earn, the rent you did not collect on your own building, the interest your own capital did not make.

Accountants count explicit costs. Economists count both. That is the entire difference between accounting profit (revenue − explicit) and economic profit (revenue − explicit − implicit), and it is why a business can be "profitable" and still worth shutting down.

Rule 2 — Only count what actually changes

If a cost happens whether or not you choose the thing, it is not part of the opportunity cost of choosing it. Food and housing while you are in college are the classic case: you eat either way, so ordinary living expenses are not a cost of attending. Only the difference counts — if dorm housing costs $4,000 more than living at home would, that $4,000 is in.

Rule 3 — Sunk costs are gone, so ignore them

A sunk cost is already spent and unrecoverable. It cannot be an opportunity cost of a future decision, because no future choice can get it back. You paid $60 for a concert ticket, you now feel ill: the $60 is gone in both worlds. The only live question is whether a miserable evening out beats a comfortable evening in.

Foundation 4 — Marginal thinking

chunk 5 of 6

Decisions are rarely all-or-nothing. They are "a bit more or a bit less," and the rule for finding the right amount is the most reusable tool in the course.

Marginal means "one more unit." Marginal benefit is the extra benefit from one more unit; marginal cost is the extra cost. Thinking at the margin means asking "is the next one worth it?" rather than "is this activity worth doing at all?"

The rule

Keep going while MB > MC. Stop where MB = MC. That point maximises net benefit — total benefit minus total cost — and it is the same rule that will later give you profit maximisation, the optimal consumption bundle, and the efficient quantity in a market.

Two wrong stopping rules that look right
  • "Keep going while total benefit is still rising." Total benefit keeps rising long after each extra unit has stopped being worth its cost. This rule massively overshoots.
  • "Stop where marginal benefit is highest." MB is usually highest at the very first unit, so this rule tells you to do almost nothing.

Foundation 5 — Trade creates value

chunk 6 of 6

The least intuitive foundation, because ordinary language treats deals as win–lose. They aren't.

Voluntary trade creates value. Nothing new is manufactured in the moment of exchange, yet both sides end up better off. That is not a paradox — value is not a property of an object, it is a relationship between an object and a person. Move the object to the person who values it more and total value has gone up.

So trade is positive-sum: both parties gain, or at minimum neither loses, because either party could have walked away. This is why "if I gained, they must have lost" is wrong, and why it is wrong for international trade too.

Where the gains come from

Trade is worth doing when people value things differently — usually because they can produce things at different opportunity costs. That is comparative advantage, and it is the whole of Chapter 2. Keep the shape of it in mind now: each side specialises where its own opportunity cost is lowest, then trades for the rest.
