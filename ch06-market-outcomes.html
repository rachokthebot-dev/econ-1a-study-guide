Surplus is the gap between value and price

chunk 1 of 4

This is Chapter 1's single buyer and seller, scaled up to a whole market. The curves you already know turn out to be lists of valuations.

TermIsOn the diagram
Willingness to payThe most a buyer would give up for a unit The height of the demand curve at that unit
Consumer surplusWillingness to pay − price actually paid Area under demand and above the price
Willingness to acceptThe least a seller would take, i.e. their cost The height of the supply curve at that unit
Producer surplusPrice received − cost Area above supply and below the price
Reading the curves as valuations

The demand curve slopes down because buyers are sorted: the keenest buyer is served first, and each later unit goes to someone who values it less. Supply slopes up for the mirror reason — the cheapest producer goes first. So the vertical gap between the curves at any unit is the value that unit creates, exactly as in Chapter 1.

Since both areas are triangles here, you can usually compute them with ½ × base × height — base is the quantity, height is the distance from the price to the curve's intercept.

01

Why the equilibrium is efficient

chunk 2 of 4

Total surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus. The competitive equilibrium maximises it, and the reason is a marginal argument you already own from Chapter 1.

Every unit where willingness to pay exceeds cost is worth making — it creates surplus. Every unit beyond that destroys surplus, because it costs more to make than anyone will pay. The quantity where the curves cross is precisely where those two conditions meet. It is MB = MC again, drawn at market scale.

Efficient is not fair

Maximum total surplus says nothing about how that surplus is split, or about who could afford to participate at all. "The market outcome is efficient" and "the market outcome is just" are different claims, and the second is normative. An exam answer that slides from one to the other is making a value judgement it has not earned.

02

Price controls and what they burn

chunk 3 of 4

A control only does anything if it binds. When it does, trades that would have created value stop happening, and that lost value is deadweight loss.

ControlBinds whenProducesExample
Price ceiling — a legal maximumSet below equilibrium A shortageRent control
Price floor — a legal minimumSet above equilibrium A surplusMinimum wage
The direction that trips people up

A ceiling is a maximum, so it only matters if it sits below where the price wanted to be — a ceiling above the equilibrium is non-binding and changes nothing. A floor is a minimum, so it bites from above. Draw the line before you reason about it and the direction stops being a guess.

Deadweight loss is the surplus that simply disappears — not transferred to anyone, just not created, because mutually beneficial trades were blocked. Watch the triangle open up.

03

What the exam actually asks

chunk 4 of 4

Four shapes, and the error each is built around.

Asked asDo thisTrap
"Calculate consumer surplus"½ × quantity × (choke price − price paid) Using the price instead of the distance from the price to the intercept.
"Is this ceiling binding?"Compare it with P*. Below → binding. Assuming every control binds. A ceiling above P* does nothing at all.
"Who gains from the ceiling?"Buyers who still get the good. Buyers shut out lose; sellers lose. Saying "consumers gain" flatly — some consumers are rationed out entirely.
"Find the deadweight loss"½ × (Q* − Q traded) × (vertical gap between the curves at Q traded) Counting a transfer as a loss. Surplus moved from sellers to buyers is not destroyed.
Transfer vs loss — the distinction that carries the marks

A binding ceiling does two different things. It transfers surplus from producers to the consumers who still buy — that surplus still exists, just in different hands. And it destroys the surplus on units no longer traded — that is the deadweight loss, and nobody receives it. Only the second is a loss to society. Confusing the two is the most common error in the whole chapter.

What's computed

ModelGenuinely computedSimplified
The two triangles Equilibrium from the linear system, then consumer and producer surplus as the exact areas between each curve and the price, and the buyers' share of the total. Linear curves, so the areas really are triangles. Curved demand needs calculus for the same idea.
Price controls Whether the control binds, the quantity traded as the short side, both surpluses at the controlled price by integration, and deadweight loss as the drop in total surplus. No black market, no queueing cost, no quality degradation — all real responses to a binding ceiling, none of them on this diagram.
