A firm that can't choose its price

chunk 1 of 4

Chapter 10 gave the firm its cost curves. This chapter puts the firm in a perfectly competitive market and asks how much it should produce, whether it should produce at all, and what happens when the whole industry has had time to adjust.

Feature of perfect competitionWhat it implies
Many buyers and sellersno single firm can move the market price
Identical productsa firm that charges more than the market price sells nothing
Free entry and exiteconomic profit attracts new firms and losses drive firms out, so profit can't last

The result is that each firm is a price taker. Its demand curve is horizontal at the market price, so every unit it sells adds exactly P to revenue: P = MR = AR. Wheat farms, commodity producers and street-food stalls in a crowded market all come close to this.

The profit-maximising rule

Produce the quantity where MR = MC, which for a price taker means P = MC, on the rising part of MC. Below that quantity the next unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so produce it. Above it, the next unit costs more than it brings in. This is Chapter 1's marginal thinking turned into a production decision.

Maximising profit ≠ minimising ATC

The firm doesn't produce where ATC is lowest. It produces where P = MC. Those two points coincide only in long-run equilibrium. Whenever price is above minimum ATC, the best output is larger than the cost-minimising one.

Losing money but still open: the shutdown rule

chunk 2 of 4

A firm earning a loss should sometimes stay open. Whether it should depends on the difference between fixed and variable costs.

Price is…Short runLong run
P > ATCproduce, earning an economic profitfirms enter
P = min ATCproduce, breaking evenno entry or exit (long-run equilibrium)
AVC ≤ P < ATCproduce at a loss: it is smaller than the fixed costfirms exit
P < min AVCshut down: produce nothing and lose only the fixed costfirms exit
  • Shutdown (short run): produce nothing for now, but still pay the fixed costs, such as the lease. Shut down if P < AVC. Fixed costs are sunk in the short run, so they don't enter the decision.
  • Exit (long run): leave the industry for good, with no costs left to pay. Exit if P < ATC.
  • So the firm's short-run supply curve is its MC curve above minimum AVC. At any price below that, it supplies zero.
The most-missed point in the chapter

"The firm is losing money, so it should shut down" is wrong whenever P is above AVC. In the model above, at $7.30 the firm loses $25.70 by producing. Shutting down would lose the full $50 of fixed cost, so producing at a loss is the better choice. Compare with AVC to decide whether to shut down. Compare with ATC to decide whether to exit.

02

Entry and exit push the price back to minimum ATC

chunk 3 of 4

In the short run the number of firms is fixed. In the long run it isn't. Free entry and exit are what make economic profit temporary.

  1. Demand rises, so the price rises, and existing firms earn an economic profit.
  2. The profit attracts new firms, which shifts market supply to the right.
  3. The price falls back, and entry continues until profit is zero.
  4. The new long-run equilibrium has the same price, P = min ATC, more firms, and a higher market quantity.

A fall in demand works the same way in reverse: losses, exit, supply shifts left, and the price recovers to minimum ATC. If input costs don't change as the industry grows (a constant-cost industry), the long-run supply curve is horizontal at minimum ATC.

Why long-run equilibrium is efficient

At P = min ATC two things hold. P = MC: the last unit is worth exactly what it costs, which is allocative efficiency. Output is at minimum ATC: goods are made at the lowest possible cost, which is productive efficiency. Competition gives consumers both without anyone planning it.

What the exam actually asks

chunk 4 of 4

Nearly every question on this chapter asks for the same four steps, in the same order.

StepDo thisTrap
1. QuantityFind q* where P = MC. Choosing where ATC is lowest, or where P = ATC.
2. Profit(P − ATC at q*) × q*, or equivalently TR − TC. Using minimum ATC instead of ATC at q*.
3. Short-run decisionCompare P with AVC: produce if P ≥ AVC. "Losing money → shut down." Check AVC, not ATC.
4. Long runProfit → entry, losses → exit, until P = min ATC and profit = 0. Saying profit lasts because the firm is efficient. Free entry removes it.
Zero profit is the destination, not a problem

In long-run equilibrium firms earn zero economic profit, but they are doing fine. Accounting profit is positive and covers the owners' opportunity costs (Chapter 10). Only economic profit is pushed to zero.
