The one distinction that decides everything

chunk 1 of 4

If you only retain one thing from this chapter, retain this. It is worth more marks than the rest of the material combined, and it is the same shift-versus-movement logic you just met on the PPC.

The rule

A change in the good's own price moves you along the curve — a change in quantity demanded or quantity supplied. Anything else shifts the whole curve — a change in demand or supply. There is no third case.

Wording you'll seeMeansOn the diagram
"quantity demanded falls"Own price roseMovement along D
"demand falls"Something other than price changedThe whole D curve shifts left
"quantity supplied rises"Own price roseMovement along S
"supply rises"Something other than price changedThe whole S curve shifts right
The circular trap

"The price of coffee rose, so demand for coffee fell, so the price fell again…" — this loop is wrong at the second step. A price rise reduces quantity demanded, which is a movement, not a shift. Only a non-price factor can shift demand. Getting this wrong turns every later question into nonsense, which is exactly why it is tested so hard.

IncomeIncome rises, for a normal good. For an inferior good it goes the other way — this reversal is a favourite.

02

Equilibrium, and what happens away from it

chunk 3 of 4

Equilibrium is the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Everywhere else the market is pushing itself back, and the direction of that push is examinable.

  • Price above equilibrium → surplus. Sellers want to sell more than buyers want to buy. Unsold stock piles up and sellers cut the price.
  • Price below equilibrium → shortage. Buyers want more than sellers will provide. Queues form and sellers raise the price.
Say it precisely

A shortage is not "not much of it about." Scarcity is permanent and applies to everything; a shortage is a specific, temporary gap between quantity demanded and quantity supplied at a particular price, and it is measured in units. Writing "there is a shortage of oil" without a price attached is the sloppy version that loses marks.

03

Working a question in a fixed order

chunk 4 of 4

Do not eyeball these. Four steps, same order every time, and the double-shift case has a known answer: one of the two outcomes is simply indeterminate.

  1. Which curve? Does the event touch buyers or sellers?
  2. Which direction? More or less at every price.
  3. Read the new crossing. Price and quantity, together.
  4. State both. Answers naming only the price lose half the marks.
When both curves move

If demand and supply both shift, one of price and quantity is determinate and the other is ambiguous — it depends on which shift is larger, which the question usually does not tell you. The correct answer names the ambiguity rather than guessing.

Both shiftPriceQuantity
D right, S rightAmbiguousRises
D left, S leftAmbiguousFalls
D right, S leftRisesAmbiguous
D left, S rightFallsAmbiguous

The pattern is worth noticing rather than memorising: when the curves shift the same direction, quantity is certain; when they shift opposite directions, price is certain.

What's computed

ModelGenuinely computedSimplified
Shift & equilibrium Both curves after your shifters, the new equilibrium by solving Qd = Qs, and the signed change in price and quantity against the starting point. Both curves are linear with fixed slopes, so only the intercepts move. Shifters are parallel shifts.
Disequilibrium Quantity demanded and supplied at your price, the gap in units, the short side that caps actual trade, and the direction of price pressure. No adjustment dynamics — the model shows the gap, not how many weeks it takes to close.
