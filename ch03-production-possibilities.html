The curve that draws scarcity

One picture holds scarcity, trade-offs, opportunity cost and efficiency at once — which is why it keeps coming back all quarter. The marks are in reading points off it correctly and knowing what moves the curve versus what moves you along it.

Four kinds of point

chunk 1 of 4

The production possibilities curve shows every combination of two goods an economy can make with all its resources and current technology. Most marks come from classifying a point correctly.

Where the point sitsCalledWhat it means
On the curveEfficient & attainable Every resource is used and used well. More of one good requires less of the other.
Inside the curveInefficient but attainable Something is idle — unemployment, an unused factory. More of both is possible.
Outside the curveUnattainable Not reachable today. It needs the curve itself to move.
On an axisComplete specialisation All resources in one good. Efficient, and usually a terrible idea.
Efficient ≠ best

Every point on the curve is efficient, and the curve has infinitely many points. Economics cannot tell you which one to pick — that needs preferences, and preferring one is a normative claim. "Efficient" means no waste, not "right."

01

Why the curve bows outward

chunk 2 of 4

A straight PPC means constant opportunity cost. A bowed one means it rises — and the reason is worth a sentence in any written answer.

Resources are not equally suited to both goods. Move the first workers from bread to laptops and you move the ones who were bad at baking anyway — cheap. Keep going and you start conscripting your best bakers, who give up a lot of bread per laptop gained. Each extra laptop costs more bread than the last. That is increasing opportunity cost, and it is what bends the line into a curve.

02

What moves the whole curve

chunk 3 of 4

Moving along the curve is a choice. Moving the curve is growth. Exams test the difference constantly, and they love technology that only helps one good.

  • More resources — workers, capital, discovered reserves. Shifts the whole curve out.
  • Better technology — may shift both intercepts or just one. A better oven moves the bread axis and leaves the laptop axis exactly where it was.
  • Trade — does not shift your PPC. It lets you consume beyond it, which is a different claim and a frequent exam trap.

The second model separates balanced growth from one-sided growth, which is where the marks hide.

03

What the exam actually asks

chunk 4 of 4

Four shapes, and the error each is hunting for.

Asked asDo thisTrap
"Classify point Z"On / inside / outside. Say what each implies. Calling an inside point "unattainable" — it is attainable and wasteful.
"Opportunity cost of moving A→B"Subtract: how much of the other good was lost. Giving the new total rather than the change.
"Does this shift the curve?"New resources or technology → yes. A different choice → no. Treating trade as a shift. It moves consumption, not production possibilities.
"Why is it bowed?"Resources are not equally suited to both goods. Answering "because of scarcity" — true of the whole curve, not its shape.
Carry this into Chapter 4

Shift-versus-movement is the exact distinction that decides supply-and-demand questions next chapter. There, a change in the good's own price moves you along a curve; anything else shifts it. Same logic, new axes.

What's computed

ModelGenuinely computedSimplified
The frontier The curve itself, the maximum bread at any laptop count, the opportunity cost of the next unit by differencing the frontier, and the classification of your point. A quarter-ellipse stands in for the real frontier. Real economies have kinked, lumpy ones.
Growth Both intercepts after resource and technology changes, the shift type, and whether a fixed previously-unreachable point is now inside the new frontier. Resource growth scales both goods equally; technology here affects one good only.
