Why the worse producer still gets the job

The one chapter where the right answer offends common sense: a country that is worse at making everything still has something worth specialising in, and both sides end up with more. The arithmetic is short, and the exam tests it relentlessly.

00

Two different advantages

chunk 1 of 4

Nearly every lost mark in this chapter comes from confusing these two. Get the distinction solid here and the rest is arithmetic.

TermMeansMeasured by
Absolute advantage Producing more of a good with the same resources Output per worker-hour. Just compare the two numbers.
Comparative advantage Producing a good at a lower opportunity cost What you give up of the other good. Requires a ratio, not a comparison.
The result that decides the chapter

One party can hold absolute advantage in everything. No party can hold comparative advantage in everything — it is arithmetically impossible, because opportunity costs are reciprocals. If you are the cheaper producer of laptops in forgone avocados, you are necessarily the dearer producer of avocados in forgone laptops. Trade follows comparative advantage, so there is always something for each side to do.

A surgeon may be the fastest typist in the hospital. She still does not type her own notes, because an hour of her typing costs an hour of surgery. Absolute advantage in typing, comparative advantage in surgery — and the slower typist gets the work.

Computing opportunity cost from a table

chunk 2 of 4

This is the mechanical skill the exam actually grades. Two goods, two producers, four numbers — and a division you must do in the right direction.

The rule, and how to not invert it

For the opportunity cost of one laptop, put laptops underneath: avocados ÷ laptops. The good you are pricing goes on the bottom. Say it out loud — "cost of one laptop, so laptops on the bottom" — because inverting this is the single most common error in the chapter, and it flips which country should specialise in what.

02

Specialise, then trade

chunk 3 of 4

Knowing who should make what is half the answer. The other half is the exchange rate between the two goods, and the fact that only a band of rates leaves both sides better off.

Each side specialises where its opportunity cost is lower, then swaps. The exchange rate — how many avocados buy one laptop — is the terms of trade, and it must sit between the two opportunity costs. Outside that band one side would rather make the good itself, so it refuses, exactly like the price slider in Chapter 1.

Why the band is where it is

Ridge gives up 2 avocados to make a laptop, so it will not sell one for less than 2. Vale gives up 4, so it will not pay more than 4. Any rate strictly between 2 and 4 beats self-sufficiency for both. The two opportunity costs are the floor and the ceiling — that is all the band is.

But a rate inside the band is necessary, not sufficient: the volume matters too. Move both sliders and watch the "both gain" verdict flip.

03

What the exam actually asks

chunk 4 of 4

Four recurring question shapes, and the error each one is hunting for.

Asked asDo thisTrap being set
"Who has absolute advantage?" Compare raw output. No division. Students divide anyway and answer the comparative question.
"Who has comparative advantage in X?" other ÷ X for both. Lower wins. Inverting the ratio, which reverses the answer.
"Give an acceptable terms of trade" Any number strictly between the two opportunity costs. Quoting a rate equal to one cost — that side is indifferent, so it isn't a gain.
"Can both gain if one is better at everything?" Yes. Say why: comparative advantage cannot be held in both goods. Answering "no" from intuition.
Three more things that cost marks
  • Units. "2 avocados per laptop" and "0.5 laptops per avocado" are the same fact. State which good you are pricing or the answer is unmarkable.
  • Identical opportunity costs. If both sides give up the same amount, there is no comparative advantage and no gain from trade. This case appears on exams specifically to see whether you noticed.
  • More output ≠ gains from trade. Gains come from reallocating production toward lower-cost producers, not from anyone working harder.
