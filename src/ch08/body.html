A control only bites on the wrong side of equilibrium

chunk 1 of 4

Chapter 6 showed that a price control burns surplus. This chapter is about what happens next: who ends up with the goods, why the damage grows over time, and what the minimum wage does to a labour market.

The names are backwards from your intuition

A ceiling is a maximum price, but to bind it has to sit below equilibrium. A ceiling above equilibrium is a cap nobody hits. Students mix this up by picturing a ceiling as something high up. Think of it as a limit on how high the price can go: it only matters if the price wants to go higher.

Both controls cut the quantity traded below equilibrium, for the same reason. At a price that is not the equilibrium price, the quantity that changes hands is the smaller of Qd and Qs. You cannot force a seller to sell or a buyer to buy.

01

When price can't ration, something else does

chunk 2 of 4

A shortage doesn't stay a gap on a graph. There are more buyers than goods, so something decides who gets them. If the price can't do it, other things will.

  • Waiting and search. Lines at the gas station, months on an apartment hunt. Time spent waiting is a real cost that nobody receives, so it eats into the gain buyers were promised.
  • Black markets. The units that are supplied are worth more to buyers than the legal price. With a ceiling at $30, the 40th unit is worth $60 to someone. That gap pulls trade off the books, and the model above computes the price from the demand curve at Qs.
  • Falling quality. A landlord who can't raise the rent can still cut maintenance. Quality falls until the real price matches the legal one.
  • Favouritism and side payments. When sellers pick from a queue, they choose friends, tenants who look easy, or whoever pays "key money" under the table.
The control does more damage over time

Supply and demand are both more elastic in the long run. Landlords convert apartments to condos and stop building. Renters who would have moved out or found roommates stay in big apartments instead. So the same rent ceiling causes a small shortage in year one and a much larger one in year ten. Go back to the model and tick long run. At a $30 ceiling the shortage grows from 30 units to 75, and the deadweight loss grows from $300 to $750.

"The market will adjust back to equilibrium"

It can't. Under a binding control the price is not allowed to move, and the price is what would close the gap. A shortage under a ceiling lasts as long as the law does. The only thing that changes over time is elasticity, and that makes the shortage worse.

02

The minimum wage is a price floor on labour

chunk 3 of 4

In the labour market the roles are swapped: workers supply labour and firms demand it. A minimum wage above the market wage is a binding floor, so it creates a surplus of labour, which is unemployment.

  • Employment falls to the quantity of labour demanded. Firms hire fewer workers, substitute machines for labour, or cut hours.
  • Unemployment = Ls − Ld. It is larger than the number of jobs lost, because the higher wage also draws new people into looking for work.
  • Whether low-wage workers earn more in total depends on the elasticity of labour demand. This is the total-revenue test from Chapter 5 with workers as the sellers. If demand is inelastic, the wage bill rises. If it is elastic, the wage bill falls.

Agricultural price supports are the same kind of floor in a goods market. To keep the floor from collapsing, governments often buy the surplus, so taxpayers pay for output that nobody consumes at that price.

What the exam actually asks

chunk 4 of 4

Written questions on price controls follow a few fixed patterns. Each pattern has one point graders look for.

Asked asDo thisTrap
"Is the control binding?"Compare it with P*. Ceiling below, floor above. Then say why in one sentence. A bare "yes" with no comparison to equilibrium gets half marks at best.
"Find the shortage / surplus"Plug the controlled price into both equations. Gap = |Qd − Qs|. Subtracting from Q* instead of from the other curve.
"Find the deadweight loss"Q traded = the short side. DWL = ½ × (D(Q) − S(Q)) × (Q* − Q). Using the controlled price as the triangle's height. The height is the gap between the curves at the traded quantity.
"Who gains, who loses?"Ceiling: sellers lose. Buyers who get the good gain, buyers who are shut out lose. Floor: the mirror image. Saying "consumers gain" from a ceiling as a group. That can be false.
"What happens over time?"Both curves become more elastic, so the shortage or surplus and the DWL grow. "The market adjusts back to equilibrium." The price isn't allowed to.
Writing a full-credit answer

For each lettered part, give the claim, the mechanism and, if there are numbers, the number. "Buyers bear more (claim) because demand is inelastic, so they can't switch away (mechanism)." Graders give points for the because. The questions at the end of this chapter are in the same format as the exam and come with a points rubric.

Transfer vs loss, again

A ceiling moves surplus from sellers to the buyers who still get the good, and it destroys the surplus on trades that stopped. Waiting in line and searching are also lost, because nobody receives that time. Bribes and key money are transfers. Keep those two categories separate in every answer.
