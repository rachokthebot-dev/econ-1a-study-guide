Responsiveness, in one number

chunk 1 of 4

Price elasticity of demand is how much quantity responds to price — expressed as a ratio of percentage changes, so the units cancel and the number is comparable across any two goods.

The definition

Ed = %Δ quantity demanded ÷ %Δ price. Because demand slopes down, this is always negative — so by convention we discuss the absolute value. Write |Ed| and the sign stops causing trouble.

|Ed|CalledMeans
> 1ElasticQuantity responds more than proportionally. Buyers are sensitive.
< 1InelasticQuantity barely moves. Buyers are stuck.
= 1Unit elasticProportional. The knife edge where revenue peaks.
0Perfectly inelasticVertical curve. Quantity never changes.
∞Perfectly elasticHorizontal curve. Any price rise kills all sales.

What makes demand elastic

  • Close substitutes available — the single biggest driver. One brand of cereal is elastic; cereal as a category is not.
  • Luxury rather than necessity — insulin is inelastic at almost any price.
  • Large share of the budget — nobody shops around over salt.
  • More time to adjust — petrol is inelastic this week and far more elastic over five years, once people can move house or change car.
  • Narrower market definition — "Honda Civics" is elastic, "cars" much less so, "transport" less still.
01

The midpoint method

chunk 2 of 4

Ordinary percentage changes give you a different answer depending on which end you start from. The midpoint formula removes that, and it is the one the exam wants.

The formula

Divide each change by the average of the two values rather than by the starting value: %ΔQ = (Q₂−Q₁) ÷ ((Q₁+Q₂)/2), and the same for price. Then divide. Because both ends use the same denominator, going up and coming back down give the identical elasticity — which the naïve method does not.

02

Elasticity changes as you move, and revenue follows

chunk 3 of 4

The most misunderstood fact in the chapter: a straight demand curve has a constant slope but a changing elasticity. Those are different things.

High up a linear demand curve the price is large and the quantity small, so a given dollar change is a small percentage of price and a large percentage of quantity — demand is elastic. Low down the reverse holds and it is inelastic. Exactly halfway it is unit elastic.

The revenue test — worth memorising cold
If demand is…Raise the priceCut the price
Elastic (|Ed| > 1)Revenue fallsRevenue rises
Inelastic (|Ed| < 1)Revenue risesRevenue falls
Unit elasticNo changeNo change

One sentence to remember it by: revenue moves with whichever is less responsive. If quantity barely budges, price wins. Drag along the curve below and watch total revenue peak exactly where elasticity equals one.

03

The other three elasticities

chunk 4 of 4

Same ratio, different numerator or denominator. Here the sign carries the meaning, so do not discard it as you did for price elasticity of demand.

ElasticityFormulaWhat the sign tells you
Income%ΔQ ÷ %Δ income Positive → normal good. Negative → inferior good. Above +1 → luxury.
Cross-price%ΔQ of A ÷ %ΔP of B Positive → substitutes. Negative → complements. Near zero → unrelated.
Price elasticity of supply%ΔQ supplied ÷ %ΔP Positive throughout. Larger with more time and spare capacity.
Four things that cost marks
  • Slope is not elasticity. A straight line has one slope and every elasticity from ∞ down to 0 along its length.
  • Dropping the sign on cross-price or income elasticity. There the sign is the answer — it is only price elasticity of demand where we take the absolute value.
  • "Elastic" is not "big demand." It is about responsiveness, not volume.
  • Using the naïve formula. If the question says midpoint, averaging is required, and the two methods give visibly different numbers.
Carry this into Chapter 7

Tax incidence is this chapter with a wedge: whichever side is less elastic ends up paying more of a tax, for exactly the reason the revenue test works — the side that cannot easily walk away absorbs the change.
