Two kinds of profit

chunk 1 of 4

This is Chapter 1's opportunity-cost foundation applied to a business. An accountant counts the cheques the business writes. An economist also counts what the owner gave up to run it.

  • Explicit costs are payments actually made: wages, rent, ingredients, utilities.
  • Implicit costs are opportunity costs with no payment attached: the salary the owner could earn elsewhere, and the interest or return their own capital could earn elsewhere.
  • Accounting profit = total revenue − explicit costs.
  • Economic profit = total revenue − explicit costs − implicit costs.
Zero economic profit is not failure

Zero economic profit means the business earns exactly what the owner's time and money would earn in their next-best use. This is called a normal profit. The accounting profit is still positive, because it is the payment for those inputs. This idea is the key to Chapter 11's long run.

Diminishing marginal product, and where cost curves come from

chunk 2 of 4

In the short run at least one input is fixed. For a bakery that is the oven and the kitchen. The firm can add workers but can't add ovens. That one fact decides the shape of every cost curve.

  • Marginal product (MP) = the change in output from one more worker. The first few workers raise each other's productivity by specialising. After that, each new worker has less of the fixed oven to work with, and MP falls. This is the law of diminishing marginal product.
  • Marginal cost (MC) = the change in total cost ÷ the change in output = wage ÷ MP. MC is the mirror image of MP. When MP falls, MC rises.
Diminishing ≠ negative

Diminishing marginal product means each extra worker adds less output, not that output falls. Total output keeps rising as long as MP is positive. A falling total requires a negative MP, which is a different and much rarer situation.

The family of cost curves, and the long run

chunk 3 of 4

Most questions on this chapter come down to six definitions and one relationship.

CurveFormulaShape and why
Fixed cost (FC)doesn't vary with Qflat: paid even at zero output
Variable cost (VC)rises with Qzero at Q = 0
Total cost (TC)FC + VCstarts at FC, not at zero
AFCFC ÷ Qalways falling: the same fixed cost spread thinner
AVC, ATCVC ÷ Q, TC ÷ QU-shaped. The gap between them is AFC, which shrinks as Q grows
MCΔTC ÷ ΔQrises because of diminishing marginal product
The one relationship to know cold

MC crosses AVC and ATC at their minimum points. It's the same as your GPA. If this quarter's grade (the marginal one) is below your average, the average falls. If it's above, the average rises. So the average is lowest exactly where the marginal equals it.

In the long run every input is variable. The bakery can build a second oven or a whole factory. The long-run average total cost (LRATC) curve traces the lowest-cost plant size for each output level:

Region of LRATCWhat's happeningWhy
Economies of scaleLRATC falls as output growsspecialisation, bulk buying, spreading big fixed investments
Constant returnsLRATC flatdoubling all inputs doubles output
Diseconomies of scaleLRATC risescoordination and management problems in very large firms
Two different causes

Diminishing marginal product is a short-run idea: one input changes while another is fixed. Diseconomies of scale is a long-run idea: every input grows together. Exam questions often use one to bait an answer about the other.

What the exam actually asks

chunk 4 of 4

Most questions on this chapter ask you to fill in a cost table or explain the shape of a curve.

Asked asDo thisTrap
"Compute economic profit"Revenue − explicit − every implicit cost: forgone salary and forgone return on capital. Counting the owner's living expenses, or forgetting the forgone interest.
"Fill in the cost table"TC = FC + VC first, then divide by Q for the averages. MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ between rows. Computing MC as TC ÷ Q. That's ATC.
"Why does MC rise?"Diminishing marginal product of the variable input, with another input fixed. Answering "diseconomies of scale". That's the long run.
"Why is ATC U-shaped?"Falling AFC pulls it down at first. Rising AVC from diminishing MP pulls it up later. Forgetting that AFC falls the whole way.
"Where does MC cross ATC?"At the minimum of ATC. Explain it with the average-and-marginal (GPA) logic. Saying MC crosses at ATC's maximum, or that the two never meet.
Fixed costs and decisions

Fixed costs are already committed in the short run, so they shouldn't change how much the firm produces today. That depends only on marginal cost. This sunk-cost logic comes from Chapter 1, and it is what Chapter 11's shutdown rule is built on.
